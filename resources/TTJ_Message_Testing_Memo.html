Don’t Stop At Weak Guardrails: Voters Across Parties Want Strong AI Protections.

TechTonic Justice
Five rounds of public opinion research, March–July 2026

You should know…

As an organization on the frontlines of the fight against AI injustice, we at TechTonic Justice know that people often think of “AI” in terms of chatbots, deepfakes, killer robots, and possible job loss. What isn’t as well known is how much AI has already infiltrated major decisions being made about our lives right now:

  • whether a health insurer approves a life-saving medical treatment;
  • whether a Social Security case gets falsely flagged as fraud; or
  • whether a landlord raises the rent.

These are not future risks. As we’ve previously shown, powerful people are already using these technologies to make decisions, mostly invisibly, about people who have no idea automated systems were involved.

Across five rounds of polling totaling 5,824 respondent interviews with likely voters, one finding held up again and again across party lines: there is very strong support for robust protections from harmful uses of AI. Every one of the following measures cleared 70% support among likely voters across all parties:

  • bans on the highest-stake uses of AI in health, housing, or work;
  • requiring makers and users of AI to prove it is harmless before it is used;
  • mandatory off switches to immediately stop harmful AI;
  • meaningful penalties for use of AI that harms; and
  • community boards with legal authority to protect the public from AI.

The support makes sense in light of the stakes. When powerful people use AI to decide fundamental issues about our individual and collective well-being, wishy-washy “guardrails” don’t offer protection or security.

We designed this polling to build around research that has come before. Most polling on AI asks people how they feel about AI as a whole and whether they want it regulated. This helps us understand general attitudes about AI and “regulation” as a concept. Our polling is more specific. We tested real-world uses of AI, the ground-level harms those systems cause, and specific policies to address those harms. Knowing how people react to these things gives us a better idea of how to build public support for proposals that prevent, protect against, and repair the harms.

This document lays out more of what five rounds of research found and what it means, whether that work happens in a legislature, a courtroom, or a community meeting.

The Core Finding and Recommendation

Voters are concerned with the concrete harms AI enables when individuals or institutions use automated systems to wield power over an important part of someone’s life. Voters consistently found more abstract concerns about AI to be less compelling across this first tranche of public opinion research than tangible harms to the ability to get, keep, or afford health care, public benefits (like Social Security), housing, or a job.

Recommendation: Make the abstract concrete and make the invisible harm visible.

People don’t necessarily think about healthcare, benefits, and housing when they think about harmful AI--they may not even be aware that automated systems are making these decisions. That’s the awareness gap to fill, discussed further below. When we show where AI is being used, who chose to use it, and what someone may lose due to it, voters respond. Be specific and human. Talk about these everyday harms as what they are: AI being used to deny people health care, a stable income, a home, and a job.

More Findings

This Is One of the Only Places that Parties Agree

Almost nothing in politics gets Democrats, independents, and Republicans to agree right now. Real limits on AI decision-making is an exception:

  • A full package of strong policy protections, including bans, off switches, proof of safety before use, penalties for harm, and community oversight boards with legal authority, drew 72% support across all voters, including 69% support among Democrats, 73% among independents, and 73% among Republicans.
  • When we asked voters what concerns they had about the policy protections, the most frequently cited concern across all parties was that the policies “might not go far enough to stop harmful AI.”
  • When we ask voters which one of the policy protections should be the highest priority, voters across all parties chose banning AI from making decisions that deny people health care, housing, or work above all others.
  • When we asked voters to rank a dozen AI concerns against each other, the same one came out on top with Democrats, independents, and Republicans: health insurers using AI to deny care doctors say patients need.

In short, “we should protect people from AI harms” is not a message that requires picking a partisan side to be effective.

Voters Support Bans and More

While bans registered as the highest priority of the individual policy protections tested, voters strongly support all of the protections listed:

  • 81% — meaningful penalties for actors that use harmful AI
  • 80% — immediate off switches for AI
  • 80% — proof of model harmlessness before release
  • 80% — bans on high-stakes uses of AI that hurt health, housing, or work
  • 75% — community oversight boards with legal authority to investigate and act on AI problems

In light of intense voter support for bold AI policy, we should not assume only voluntary or incremental measures will be publicly acceptable.

People Still Want to Reach People

85% of voters were extremely, very, or somewhat concerned about being forced to deal with AI instead of a person for customer service. Presumably, people want to be able to reach someone who can listen, explain, exercise judgment, take responsibility, and change an outcome in the way only a fellow human being can. Applying this principle to the various ongoing uses of AI decision-making discussed above, we assume that a patient wants their doctor’s judgment to matter, a worker wants to know who is evaluating their performance, and a potential tenant wants to know why their rental application was rejected.

Liking an Institution Does Not Necessarily Mean Trusting Its Use of AI

We tested the general favorability of various people and groups, including named public figures and generalized categories like landlords, employers, and health insurance companies. We then asked how upset or angry poll respondents would be if those same people and groups were in charge of AI used to make decisions about their lives.

General favorability is not necessarily associated with decreased anger about AI use. For example, 75% of employed respondents reported having a somewhat or very favorable view of their employers, significantly higher than most other people and groups we tested. Even so, 53% of employed respondents were at least somewhat upset by an employer putting AI in charge of consequential decisions.

Likely voters were also concerned about other actors using AI to make decisions about their lives: health insurers (68%), landlords (67%), private companies running public programs (67%), state government officials (66%), and Big Tech companies (64%) were all viewed as at least somewhat upsetting when put in charge of consequential AI decisions. People may like their employer or landlord, but they strongly dislike them using AI to make decisions about their lives.

Naming the institution responsible for a decision does not require attacking it. The focus can stay on the choice to use automated decision-making in a particular context.

A Caution about Bias and Discrimination

When ranked in comparison to various other arguments about AI’s harms, an argument about bias and discrimination was considered less concerning than all but one of the 12 arguments listed. This does not mean that people should avoid talking about bias and discrimination, but the finding demonstrates the need for a nuanced approach.

Other examples of AI harms, such as health companies using AI to deny care, may better resonate. Given the racial, gender, and disability biases layered into these harms, an anti-bias or anti-discrimination lens can still be incorporated when discussing them. Moreover, we have begun polling tools to effectively message around race and economic harms with respect to AI, including the race class narrative. We will share more in the near future.

The bias argument listed was this: “Companies are deploying AI trained on biased data, making discrimination against women, people of color, and disabled people worse. They bury the bias in code, making it harder to see and fight.”

Voters are concerned by real-world uses of AI that make decisions about their health, benefits, and employment.

We showed poll respondents specific, real examples of the ways powerful people use AI to make critical decisions and asked how concerned each use made them: 83% of respondents are extremely, very, or somewhat concerned about a doctor-recommended treatment being denied, 83% about Social Security or disability benefits being cut off, 81% about being rejected for a job with no human involved, 81% about being monitored at work, and 80% about being falsely accused of benefits fraud.

Between 70 and 78% of respondents were concerned by other real-world uses of AI, including to cut off Medicaid, reject apartment applications over faulty background checks, raise rents through automated systems, and falsely accuse children of being threats at school.

Voters may not be aware of the harms that concern them most.

While voters’ present knowledge of AI harms in health care, benefits, and employment was not directly tested, we have two reasons to believe that it is relatively low.

First, TechTonic Justice has trained thousands of frontline advocates, community members, policy experts, academics, and others about the ways powerful people use AI to make decisions about our lives. Both anecdotally and in internal survey responses (not the present polling), relatively few trainees are aware of the extent of these forms of ongoing harmful AI use before our training.

Second, in the course of polling, we asked likely voters an open-ended, free-response question to get a general sense of existing ways voters understand the dangers of AI: “What uses of artificial intelligence (AI) are you most concerned about affecting you or your family?” Many respondents left the field blank. Of those that responded, many defaulted to concerns they already have names for, like privacy and surveillance, job loss, and misinformation or deepfakes.

These top-of-mind concerns could change when voters are presented with other harms, like medical care being denied, benefits being cut off, job rejections with no human involved, being monitored at work, and being falsely accused of fraud. Raising awareness of these other harms will be key.

What This Means

Invisible Harm and Opportunity

We know voters are deeply concerned by ongoing AI uses that hurt our health care, work, housing, and benefits. And, voters support bold policy proposals to stop the harms. In light of this, raising voter awareness should be a priority for everyone concerned by AI.

Raising awareness about these ongoing, ground-level harms can happen multiple ways. You can talk to the communities you belong to or serve. You can share stories of injustice that you or people you know are experiencing and amplify similar stories from elsewhere. If you have capacity, you can directly take on fights to resist the harms.

Wherever you enter the fight, know that you won’t be alone. TechTonic Justice and our allies will be here to support and work alongside you. You can ask TechTonic Justice for strategic assistance, messaging ideas, or legal and organizing support in a local AI fight. You can also invite TechTonic Justice to host a training or attend a community meeting where we can discuss these issues in depth and build collective understanding of the challenges in front of us.

The moment couldn’t be more urgent. Powerful people are already using AI to intensify injustice. The technology is taking hold in governments, education, work, and housing. If we don’t stop it soon, it could be with us indefinitely.

So, we have to do something new and do it now. Existing efforts to rein in the use of the technology, though hard-fought, haven’t turned into lasting policy wins. Raising awareness of these ongoing, ground-level harms may be the best untapped opportunity to motivate the popular support needed to protect us all against AI harms and assure a more just future for everyone.

Methodology

Findings are drawn from five rounds of message testing conducted by TechTonic Justice and Data for Progress among likely voters nationally, fielded March–July 2026 (n=7,043 combined):

Round 1 (n=1,242, March 6–9): MaxDiff government use of AI in public benefits.
Round 2 (n=1,193, March 20–23): MaxDiff comparison of AI harms across institutions.
Round 3 (n=1,167, April 24–27): institutional favorability and AI decision-making authority.
Round 4 (n=1,164, July 2–5): concern ratings across fifteen concrete AI use scenarios, plus an open-ended write-in question.
Round 6 (n=1,058, July 24–27): support for specific AI policy proposals, tested in a split sample.

MaxDiff (maximum difference scaling) asks respondents to choose the most and least compelling option from repeated small sets, producing a relative-preference score; positive scores indicate a message performed above the average of all options tested, negative scores below it.

Data for Progress works with a series of web panel respondent marketplaces such as Cint to recruit web respondents. To guard against self-selection among respondents, DFP maintains a complex set of quotas and screening questions to ensure each survey is representative of a genuine sample of respondents.

Web panel survey research requires a delicate balance between disqualifying disingenuous respondents and controlling for frequent participants, while also preserving responses from genuine and low-socioeconomic status respondents. DFP monitors providers, respondents, and trends among responses to detect anomalies in respondent pools, and automatically disqualifies or controls for respondents based on a series of checks, including attention, truthfulness, inconsistency, and completion speed. DFP also works with providers to monitor quality and blacklist those with high failure rates or inconsistent respondents to ensure accuracy.

Our national omnibus surveys are weighted to match the composition of likely voters by age, gender, education, race, geography, recalled vote, and select joint distributions of those variables from the TargetSmart commercially available voter file and high-quality, third-party turnout modeling.
